01

What you saw, and why

Three screenshots, three separate root causes. None of them were cosmetic.

“Whats the issue here?” — a broken image in the Review card

Cause

.stage { display: flex } in practice/index.astro has no [hidden] guard, so it out-cascades the attribute's display: none. The Review stage therefore renders on every page load, drill mode included — and its <img id="review-img" alt="Case diagram"> ships with no src at all. Chrome paints exactly the broken glyph plus alt text you photographed. A second, independent bug piles on: when a case id in the review queue isn't in caseById, nextReview() returns early — before setting either the image or the name.

Fix shipped

A global [hidden] { display: none !important } in tokens.css — so no future display: rule can ever defeat the attribute again — plus: never server-render a visible src-less image, clear a stale src when the next case has no diagram, and purge orphan cards from the queue instead of dead-ending on them.

“Why do we have white outlines on the cube? The image feels disconnected and not integrated in the text.”

Cause

Two problems wearing one coat. First, all 130 generated SVGs bake in <rect fill="#FFFFFF" rx="6"> — correct for the print PDF, an 18.4:1 white slab on the app's dark theme and a card-inside-a-card on light. Second, the diagrams have no placement: they're bare <img width="60"> tags dropped into an 820 px prose column with display: inline, margin: 0, no frame, no caption and no height attribute. The lesson layout styles paragraphs, lists and code — and nothing else.

Fix shipped + planned

The generator now emits a self-contained @media (prefers-color-scheme) block per file, so one SVG serves both themes and the PDF path (typst's renderer skips @media and gets the light default). Next: a Figure.astro that gives every diagram a real frame, a caption and a 96–120 px render size, and a 68 ch measure so the picture and the sentence it illustrates occupy the same column.

“Why are we showing the solution with the white side above and not the yellow side as it should be? Shouldn't we only highlight the parts that the alg solves? Can't we gray out the rest?”

Cause

cubing.js hard-codes its palette per axis — U = white, F = green, R = red — and exposes no colour-scheme API (color-scheme is light/dark chrome, not face colours). Cubepath teaches yellow-up, red-front, green-right. Nothing in TwistyPlayer.astro reconciled them. And the coarse experimental-stickering="OLL" preset does assume the whole layer is in play — exactly your objection.

Fix shipped

experimental-setup-alg="x2 y" maps the fixed palette onto the course's scheme. That rotation silently inverts every built-in stickering (masks bind to the cubie, not the slot, so the OLL highlight lands on the bottom layer) — so the masks are now generated from experimental-stickering-mask-orbits, per facelet, derived at build time rather than hand-typed. Everything the algorithm doesn't touch greys out. control-panel="none" and viewer-link="none" retire the grey bar and the “Tw” watermark.

The pattern behind all three

Each bug is the same failure at a different layer: the picture and the words were built by different people who never met. The Python generator doesn't know the app has a dark theme. The lesson layout doesn't know it contains images. The 3D player doesn't know which way up the course holds the cube. Everything below is about making one system out of four.

A

One design system, not eleven stylesheets

tokens.css is 178 lines and defines seven classes. The pages then write 835 lines of scoped CSS on top of it, re-declaring .chip three times, .btn twice, .hint three times (once renamed .tiny), and main { max-width } six times with five different values. There are 22 font sizes, 27 padding values and 6 radii, all raw pixels. The comment in index.astro — “the scoped .btn color would out-cascade the global .btn.primary” — is the system telling you it has no system.

B

One case, three renderings

Cubepath is four products sharing one dataset — lessons, trainer, reference, printed cards. Each wants a different amount of the same case. Today they're configured almost identically, which is why the reference page feels heavy and the lesson feels thin.

What each tier gains

  • Tier 1 stays cheap. No 3D player in a grid of 57 OLL cases — that's the one thing the reference page must never do.
  • Tier 2 gets the teaching. A recognition ring drawn on the specific stickers you're meant to look at (today recognition is prose only), the algorithm rendered move-by-move and synced to the player, a slower tempo, and back-view="top-right" so the hidden half of the cube stops being a mystery.
  • Tier 3 gets out of the way. The drill screen should show a scramble and a timer. Everything else is a distraction until you press “Show case”.
C

Diagrams and motion

Is the Ryan's Tutorials style better than ours?

You asked directly. The honest answer: steal its pedagogy, not its implementation. Ryan's cheat sheet does one thing our player doesn't — it draws the hidden faces as detached, half-opacity ghost panels beside the cube. We render three faces of six and have hint-facelets switched off, so the learner is guessing about the half they can't see. That single idea is worth taking.

Against that: nine cases in roughly 5,000 px of scrolling, white-versus-grey stickers that are nearly indistinguishable, and no interactivity at all. Meanwhile jperm.net — the 2026 benchmark — uses zero animation: static plan-view images in a sortable table with your own recorded times in it. Neither site is the model on its own. The three-tier split above is what you get when you take Ryan's teaching density at Tier 2 and jperm's scannability at Tier 1.

Concretely, worth taking

  • Ghost panels for hidden faces — turn hint-facelets="floating" back on at Tier 2, where recognition is being taught.
  • A per-move caption track — Ryan narrates each move; we narrate nothing.
  • The algorithm as a filmstrip — each move a card, the current one highlighted as it plays. This is the single biggest legibility win available, and it uses the alg string we already have.
  • Recognition rings — draw the cue on the diagram instead of describing it in a sentence.
  • jperm's sortable table with your times in it — we already store per-case FSRS data and never surface it on the reference page.

The diagram pipeline

The white plate is fixed. The larger gap is coverage: 104 of 185 cases have no diagram at all — every F2L case (41), every 4×4 case (49) and every 5×5 L2E case (13). The reference page renders all 41 F2L tiles as empty numbered boxes. Extending fullsets.py to emit F2L slot diagrams and big-cube case diagrams is the single largest content win in the plan, and it costs no new hand-authored data — the algorithms are already verified, and the pipeline already derives sticker state from them.

A second, cheaper idea worth testing: cubing.js can render the plan view from the same alg string that drives the player. One source, two views, no sync risk — and it would make the 2D diagram and the 3D animation provably agree.

D

Navigation, progress, and the missing handoff

The site is a 25-lesson course with a trainer attached, and not one lesson links to the trainer. That's the navigation problem in a sentence.

Give the learner a position

There is no progress indication anywhere — despite an IndexedDB status store the course never reads. The homepage is a hero plus 25 links in five cards; nothing marks what you've done, and the CTA always points at lesson one even on your twentieth visit.

  • A lessonProgress store, marked complete when the pager's “next” is used.
  • Per-phase n/m bars on the homepage, a ✓ on finished lessons, and a hero CTA that reads “Resume: Permute the corners” when there's history.
  • “Step 4 of 25 · Phase 1” beside the lesson breadcrumb.
  • All of it client-side, so the static build is unaffected and the PWA still works offline.

Close the loop to practice

Every lesson ends with a ## Practice block that deep-links into the trainer with the right set preselected — /practice/?group=2look-pll-edges. A course that teaches four algorithms and then doesn't hand you the drill for them is doing half its job.

Surface the things that already exist

Three of the four deliverables — the PDF guide, the printable card set, the print page — are reachable only from one sentence buried in the reference page's lead paragraph. Add Print as a fourth top-level destination and a “Take it offline” block on the homepage. Add the theme toggle the [data-theme] block was written for and nothing ever sets.

F

The lessons are not too long. They are under-illustrated.

Worth saying plainly, because “lots of text” has two possible cures and only one is right here. The 25 lessons total 13,484 words — about 10,700 of prose. Measured against the project's own stated philosophy (“as small and concise as possible while containing all information needed”), the writing is genuinely terse and genuinely good. Six lessons carry zero visual elements. Four more carry one. That's the actual defect: prose is narrating motion that a diagram or a player should demonstrate.

One thing outside the code

CI is advisory today — there is no branch protection on master, while every push to master auto-deploys to production. A branch ruleset requiring the existing checks is the cheapest risk reduction available in this whole document.

Four calls that are yours, not mine

  1. How far does the visual identity move?

    Everything above is a repair of the existing warm-paper / Newsreader / IBM Plex identity, not a replacement. If you want the site to look genuinely different rather than genuinely finished, that's a separate and larger decision — say so and I'll treat the palette and type as open.

  2. Do the 104 missing diagrams get built?

    It's the largest content win available and it needs no new hand-authored data — but it's real work in fullsets.py, and F2L slot diagrams in particular need a new diagram type. Worth it, in my view; expensive enough to be your call.

  3. Dimmed, or actually grey?

    The pieces an algorithm doesn't touch are now dimmed — dark red, olive, dark green. cubing.js also offers a neutral ignored grey, which is closer to your words. Dimming keeps the colour scheme readable while you learn recognition; grey is starker and more literal. It's one character per mask either way.

  4. Branch protection on master?

    Every push deploys to production and CI can't block anything. Turning on a ruleset changes your own workflow, so I haven't touched it.
