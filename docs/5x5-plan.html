01

What the course becomes

You asked to orient the 5×5 on J Perm's beginner tutorial, with three things to learn: switching a centre piece, switching the edges, and the edge parity algorithm. You then corrected the first one — the centre step "isn't a proper alg, it is more a learned approach." That correction is right, and the repo already agrees with it in code. So the honest count is two algorithms plus one technique.

Algorithm 1 Edge flip

Seven outer turns. Cannot break a finished pair. Identical on the 4×4.

Algorithm 2 Edge parity

The one odd generator the puzzle mathematically requires. Already shipped.

Technique — no algorithm The centre insert

A conjugate you aim. No case, no trainer entry, no drillable string.

Provenance — resolved

The video's transcript and description are now in hand, supplied directly rather than fetched. Every attribution below is first-hand and quotable; the earlier version of this document had to route everything through jperm.net/5x5 as a proxy, and that caveat is withdrawn.

The description publishes both algorithms in plain text — Edge Flip Algorithm ► R U R' F R' F' R and Parity Algorithm ► Rw U2 x Rw U2 Rw U2 3Rw' U2 Lw U2 Rw' U2 Rw U2 Rw' U2 Rw' — both byte-identical to what this plan recommends and to what the repo already ships.

Three claims in this document were second-hand and are now confirmed from the source itself: that the flip is the same algorithm on every big cube, that the centre step is explicitly not an algorithm, and that there is exactly one parity. Each is quoted where it is used.

The two algorithms

Edge flip — turns one edge group over in place verified
R U R' F R' F' R
Centres
Zero pieces visually changed. Exact cycles exist inside the U face, but they move same-colour pieces — the cube shows no centre disturbance at all.
Edges
The FR slot's two wings swap and its midge flips: the front-right group turns over as one object. The other eight wings and four midges are exactly a U turn of the four top-layer groups, intact.
Corners
A 4-cycle plus one twist. Corners are free during reduction — the 3×3 stage places them.
Safety
It is mechanically incapable of breaking a finished pair. Outer turns only, so it maps intact edge groups to intact edge groups everywhere on the cube. Verified directly, not reasoned about.
Cross-puzzle
Legal on the 4×4×4 kpuzzle too, and being outer-turn-only it means literally the same thing on both puzzles — unlike anything containing Rw.

Confirmed by the source, unprompted — at 4:15 in the video: "the algorithm to flip one edge is the same as the one you would have used for a 4×4, and it's the same as what you used for larger cubes as well." This was the plan's own claim before the transcript arrived; it is now a quotation rather than an inference.

Edge parity — the one odd generator verified · already shipped
Rw U2 x Rw U2 Rw U2 3Rw' U2 Lw U2 Rw' U2 Rw U2 Rw' U2 Rw'
Centres
Exact 6- and 8-piece permutations, zero visually changed; a whole-cube rotation brings them home. The algorithm is centre-clean.
Edges
The UF group's two wings exchange places, each turned around, while its midge stays put — that is the flipped-edge look. UR and UL swap intact.
Corners
Two swap. Harmless during reduction; the 3×3 stage cleans it up.

Half of all solves — the video, at 6:10: "there's a 50% chance you will have finished all the edges, but the other 50% of the time you'll get one flipped edge left." The hold
Load-bearing, and measured. A synthetic single-flipped-group state was built at five different slots. At UF it solves; at UB, UR, FR and DF it does not. "Hold the flipped group at UF" is an instruction, not decoration.
Provenance
Already pinned as 5x5-edge-parity in case-states.json, already verified by verify-l2e.mjs, already printed on the cheat card, already asserted token-for-token against the 4×4 form in test_notation.py. Nothing new needs to be trusted.
Rejected — both destroy 5×5 centres

Ruwix publishes Rw U2 Rw' U2 Rw U2 Rw U2 Lw' U2 Rw U2 Rw' U2 x' U2 Rw2 as the 5×5 parity algorithm, claiming it also serves the 4×4. Measured: 8 of the 24 "+" centre pieces visually changed and the fixed centres 4-cycle. No whole-cube rotation brings them home. The 4×4 OLL-parity form fails identically.

Both differ from the correct string by exactly the token the repo already pins — Rw' versus 3Rw' at index 7. Do not cite ruwix as corroboration for parity. It is fine for the edge flip, where it agrees character-for-character and the layer-count trap cannot apply.

This is the concrete stake for the lesson's caution callout: the wrong algorithm does not shred your paired edges, it shreds your centres, unrecoverably.

03

The centre insert, as taught technique

The source says this outright — 3:15, quoted verbatim

"I'm gonna teach you a trick that can bring any piece from up here down into the front… make sure you remember this whole pattern — it's not necessarily one algorithm, but this pattern will apply no matter which pieces you're trying to move into here."

Your correction was not a softening of the source, it was the source. J Perm then walks two different worked instances of the same pattern rather than one string, which is exactly the shape §03 recommends. There is no centre algorithm anywhere in the video — the earlier finding stands, now from the primary text.

Your description — align the piece, move it down where it belongs, move it away, bring the column down, put the face back, move the layer up, return — is a conjugate: take away, act, put back. It also tracks the video's own narration beat for beat. The repo already ships exactly this shape one puzzle down. 444-centers.mdx carries algorithms: [], no case row, and a single figure: Rw U Rw', captioned "lift a column of the front centre into the top, turn the top, lower it home."

Why the conjugate restores what it disturbs

Rw carries a whole column away, U only touches the top layer, Rw' carries the column back. Every piece the grab moved that the middle beat did not touch returns to the exact slot it left. The only pieces that end up somewhere new are the one you wanted to move and the one you displaced to make room.

Measured on the 5×5, so the claim survives the extra layer

Rw U Rw' — visually changed: U+F and F+R (+ centres), UxFL and FxRU (X centres). Nothing else. The R, L, B and D centres are untouched: all four finished faces come back exactly as they were.

M U M' — visually changed: U+R, U+L, B+U, B+D (+ centres only); X centres untouched. This is the 5×5's extra grab — the middle column of a centre grid does not exist on a 4×4, and the two-wide grab cannot reach it.

How to write it

  1. The invariant in two sentences — take away, act, put back.
  2. One worked instance on one concrete position, Rw U Rw', labelled explicitly as one instance of the pattern, not the algorithm, and carrying the measured guarantee: the R, L, B and D centres come back untouched. That is what makes a learner trust it.
  3. The recognition that generalises it: which layer you grab is chosen by which column the piece must land in; the middle beat is chosen by what you want on top.
  4. The 5×5-only note that the middle column needs the middle slice.

Frontmatter keeps algorithms: [] — that field holds case ids and there is no case here. But the copy must say the quiet part out loud, once: "There is no algorithm on this page. Centres are a pattern you aim, not a sequence you recall." The site otherwise reads an empty algorithms: as "nothing to learn here", which is the opposite of true for the step that is half the solve.

No CaseDef, no trainer case, no drillable entry. Practice for centres is already correctly wired as a trainer note — scramble, solve all six centres, scramble again.

04

Is the cut safe?

This is the question that matters, and it deserves an answer from the puzzle's structure rather than from deference. Yes — and it is provable rather than customary. Two independent lines.

The invariant: why exactly two, not one and not three

Parity of the permutation induced on the 24 wings
GeneratorWing parity
Any outer turn (U, R, F …)even
R U R' F R' F' R — the flipeven
The flip conjugated by Uweven
The flip conjugated by Rweven
The parity algorithmodd

Conjugation cannot change permutation parity. So every slice-flip-slice — any setup, any slice, any repetition, interleaved with any outer turns — is even on wings. A reduced cube is necessarily even on wings. Therefore an odd-wing-parity state is unreachable by any amount of pairing technique, and the parity algorithm is not a convenience: it is the second generator the puzzle requires. Conversely nothing beyond it is required, because one application flips the class and everything left over is even.

This is the best sentence available for the lesson

"Everything you do while pairing swaps wings in pairs. Parity is the state where one swap is left over — and no amount of pairing can produce an odd number of swaps."

It replaces the current lesson's hand-wavy "wings have no orientation of their own", which is a weaker claim about a different thing.

The construction: actual solutions were found

Withdrawn — this is what implementation found

A planning agent reported that all 13 cases fall to at most three slice-flip-slice macros, and this document said so. Turning that into a regression test did not reproduce it: an 850-macro vocabulary searched to depth 2 solves 7 of the 13 — l2e-1, 2, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11. The other six may need a third macro or a better aiming vocabulary; that was not established either way.

A lesson sentence briefly read "at most three rounds finishes any case — that is measured, not an estimate." It was not measured. It is gone.

Nothing about the cut changes. What makes it safe was never a bound on how many times a learner fires the flip — it is the necessity proof above, which holds, plus the fact that the flip is the standard pairing tool. The table below is what shipped as a test.

Every case has one parity — every alternate algorithm of a case agrees with it — and one application of the parity algorithm clears every odd case. That is the sharp form of what the lesson rests on: parity is the only obstruction the flip cannot pass, and it is asserted per case rather than argued.

The rest of the solve, checked structurally

  • Centres — no parity exists on an odd cube, and the conjugate technique reaches every centre piece, so centres always finish.
  • The 3×3 stage — a 5×5 has a true fixed centre on every face, so midges and corners obey 3×3 law and there is no PLL parity. The existing lesson already says this and is correct.
  • No mirror form is needed — any group can be rotated to FR, and the parity algorithm's single hold is reachable by cube rotation.
Two things that are close to being a fourth item, but are not

(a) The parity hold is UF and only UF. Recognition is load-bearing, so the lesson must state it.
(b) On a 5×5 the centre conjugate needs a choice of grab — two-wide versus the middle slice — that the 4×4 never needed. One technique, two grabs; not a new algorithm.

What is lost is speed, never the ability to finish. The 13-case set is a one-look optimisation; the beginner path replaces it with an iterative loop of up to three slice-flip-slices. That is a time cost.

Correction to an earlier claim of mine

09

Making the technique drillable

Your idea: a drill that hands the learner a random centre-piece position and makes them adapt the pattern to it. It is buildable, it is the right instinct, and it is bigger than everything else on this page put together.

Why it does not fit the existing trainer

Every trainer set today is a fixed list of cases with a stored answer, scheduled by FSRS — "recall this algorithm." A centre insert has no answer key: the whole point is that the moves change with the position. Graded as recall, it is ungradeable.

The unlock — grade the state, not the moves

We never need to know what the learner did. Generate a position, let them work, and check the resulting state against a predicate: are all six centres uniform, and is the piece that had to move now home? Any sequence that gets there is correct — which is not merely easier to implement, it is more correct for a technique with no canonical solution.

That predicate already exists in prototype: it is the centres-visually-home check I wrote for the 5×5 verification work, in scratchpad/5x5/l2e-core.mjs.

Generating positions by running the technique backwards

Do not hand-author positions — the repo's derive-everything rule applies here too. Instead: take a cube with solved centres, pick a random grab (Rw, M, 3Rw…) and a random middle beat (U, U', U2), and apply the inverse of that conjugate. The result is guaranteed solvable in exactly one conjugate, and you already know a solution to offer as a hint.

Difficulty grades itself: one conjugate, two, three. No case list, no recognition table, and an effectively unbounded supply of positions — which is precisely what a pattern needs and a case set cannot give.

Input

cubing.js 0.63.3 does expose interactive input — experimentalDragInput and experimentalMovePressInput on the player, plus experimentalAddMove to push moves in. So the learner can turn the cube directly rather than tapping a button pad. Two cautions: it is experimental* API, which we already carry risk on with the stickering mask; and dragging is awkward on a phone, so a move pad should exist as well, not instead.

What it is really worth

The reason to build this properly is that centres are not the only thing we teach as technique and cannot drill. The same machine — generate backwards, grade the state — covers F2L intuition, 4×4 edge pairing, 5×5 freeslice and both centre steps. Those are four lessons that currently end by sending the reader to a trainer that has nothing for them.

Honest cost

Position generator, state predicate, input surface, feedback and hint UI, a persistence model that is not FSRS cards, and tests for all of it. This is a new product surface, not a lesson edit. It deserves its own PR and its own design pass — and it should probably debut on the 4×4 centre step, which is the simplest instance (no middle slice) and the one with the most learners.

notation.py asserts that the big-cube chunk blocks are never compacted because "every one of them carries a layer-count prefix somewhere in the block". All four current strings satisfy that. R U R' F R' F' R has no layer-count prefix anywhere — it is the first big-cube string that would fall through to the compacted path, changing how it renders and silently invalidating the comment that explains why the block is safe.

Either opt the line out explicitly and amend the comment to say the rule is now a per-block choice, or let it compact and look at the rendered card. Either way the comment must change. Rebuild the card and inspect it — this is one of the few things a test cannot judge.

The second-order win: the card's cue "slice out, run it, slice back — both cubes" is currently attached to an algorithm whose Rw means different things on the two puzzles. Swapping in the outer-turn-only string makes that claim true for the first time.

What is not verified

The repo's value proposition is that its algorithm data is verified. The matching discipline for its sources is to record exactly which links were read and which were not. Items 1 and 2 belong in DECISIONS.md in these words, as part of the record rather than a footnote.

  1. The video was not retrieved. RESOLVED — the transcript and description were supplied. Both algorithms are confirmed byte-identical, and the lesson may now quote the source directly. This was the largest open item in the plan and it closed in our favour: every claim it touched was confirmed, none overturned.
  2. jperm.net/5x5 was second-hand. NO LONGER LOAD-BEARING — the video itself now carries the two strings and the "not necessarily one algorithm" framing. The written page remains unverified as to its exact wording, so quote the video, not the page.
  3. The hard last-two-centres cases exist only in the video. RESOLVED, and there are none — the 3:03 demonstration is the conjugate trick itself, not a set of cases with algorithms. The plan already described it correctly.
  4. The completeness search did not hold up. Re-run during implementation it solves 7 of 13, not all 13 — see §04. The necessity half is a proof and never depended on it, and the per-case parity table is now pinned across all 13. This is the one claim in this document that implementation overturned.
  5. Solvability was verified in the move group, not at the table. "At most three rounds" is a claim about existence, not about how hard the setup is to spot.
  6. No repo gates were run. No make check, no verifier, no vitest, no Playwright — this was a read-only pass with three waves live in the tree. Every regeneration and test change above is unrun.
  7. The 4×4 lesson's "flip trick" prose was not checked against the seven moves; only the string's legality on the 4×4 kpuzzle was.
  8. jperm.net/4x4 was fetched and read — unlike the 5×5 video. Its five algorithm strings and its Yau framing are first-hand. But the page embeds a video too, and anything demonstrated only in that video is still unretrieved — including, by their own cross-reference, the harder last-two-centres cases.
  9. The drill is a design, not a spike. No prototype was built. The three claims that carry it — grade-the-state, generate-backwards, and experimentalDragInput existing — are each checked, but they have never been run together.
