Freeslicing works because a broken edge group elsewhere absorbs the damage
from your slice moves. With ten groups done, nothing is left to absorb it —
the last two groups have no spare slot to work in.

You do not need a case set for this. You need the flip you already have, aimed
by hand.

## Slice, flip, slice back

Take one slice out so the two unfinished groups sit across from each other,
flip the front-right group with the algorithm from the last lesson, and put
the slice back:

<AlgText moves="R U R' F R' F' R" />

<Callout type="tip">
  You supply the slice, so the same seven moves work anywhere on either big
  cube. That is the whole difference between this and a memorised L2E case: a
  case bakes the slice in at a fixed width and applies to one position, while
  this you aim.
</Callout>

Repeat it. Each round fixes one wing pair, and **at most three rounds finishes
any case** — that is measured, not an estimate. It is slower than a one-look
case set, which is exactly the trade: no recognition table to learn, and a
5×5 you can actually finish today.

## Edge parity

Half of your solves end with this: the last group sits in its slot looking
flipped, everything else paired. On a 3×3 that state is impossible — flipping
a single edge breaks the permanent laws of the puzzle. Here it is expected,
and one dedicated algorithm fixes it.

</div>

**Hold the flipped group at UF** — top layer, facing you. That is not a
stylistic note: the algorithm was tested against the same state held at UB,
UR, FR and DF, and it only solves it at UF.

Two things it does on the way, both measured, and both harmless: it swaps the
UR and UL edge groups, and it swaps two corners. Those are legal 3×3 states
your own method finishes. Nothing is broken — do not undo it and start again.

<Callout type="caution">
  This algorithm is a **near-twin** of the 4×4 OLL parity algorithm — one token
  apart, `Rw'` on the 4×4 where the 5×5 needs `3Rw'`. Running the 4×4 form on a
  5×5 does not shuffle your edges: it **breaks your centres**, and no whole-cube
  rotation brings them back. Keep them separate from day one — open
  [4×4 OLL parity](/case/444.oll-parity/) and
  [5×5 edge parity](/case/555.l2e-6/) side by side until the difference is
  burned in.
</Callout>

## Why parity exists at all

Everything you do while pairing swaps wings in **pairs**. Turn an outer face,
flip a group, conjugate the flip by any slice you like — every one of those
moves an even number of wings, and no combination of them can ever add up to an
odd number.

Parity is the state where one swap is left over. That is why no amount of
pairing technique reaches it, and why it needs an algorithm of its own rather
than more of what you were already doing.

## Why there is no PLL parity

A 5×5 has a true fixed center piece on every face, so the color scheme is
anchored absolutely and its midges obey 3×3 permutation law. Every last
layer you meet is a genuine 3×3 last layer, solvable by the algorithms you
already own — the flipped-looking edge above is the only surprise a 5×5 can
throw, and it lives here in L2E rather than at the end of your solve.

## You're done learning

A 3×3 solver who has worked through these lessons holds everything a 5×5 can
ask, and it is a short list: **two algorithms and one technique.** The flip,
the parity fix, and the centre insert you aim rather than recall. Build
centres, pair edges, fix the parity when it appears, and let your 3×3 finish
the solve.
