Plain reduction solves all centers, then all edges, then starts the 3×3 stage
from zero. **Yau** uses the same skills — centers, **edge pair** (two edges glued into
one, acting as a single 3×3 edge; cubers write *dedge*) pairing, parity — but
reorders them so that when reduction ends, your cross is already done.

## The five steps

1. **Two opposite centers** — build the white and yellow centers first. White
   is your cross color.
2. **Three cross pairs** — pair three of the four white-cross edge pairs and
   store them around the white center.
3. **Remaining four centers** — hold the white center and its three cross
   edge pairs on the **left**, out of the way, and build the four side centers
   without disturbing them.
4. **Last cross pair + remaining edges** — pair the fourth cross edge pair, then
   the other eight, keeping the cross on the left the entire time.
5. **3×3 stage** — bring the cross to the bottom. It's already solved — go
   straight to the white corners. Parity works exactly as before.

## Why it wins

- **The cross is free.** In plain reduction you finish pairing, then hunt for
  cross pieces like everyone else. In Yau the cross was assembled as a side
  effect — the 3×3 stage starts one full step ahead.
- **Fixed orientation.** Steps 3 and 4 happen in a single grip, cross on the
  left. You always know where every center and edge pair is without rotating to
  check — recognition and lookahead get dramatically better, and better
  lookahead is where big-cube speed lives.

<Callout type="caution">
  Don't switch too early. Building centers around protected cross edge pairs is
  genuinely awkward at first, and Yau will make you *slower* for a week or
  two. Stay on plain reduction until it's comfortable — around sub-2:30 —
  then make the jump.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
  Learn it in pieces: do steps 1–2, then finish the solve with plain
  reduction. Once storing the cross pairs feels natural, add the
  fixed-orientation center build, then the left-grip edge pairing.
</Callout>
