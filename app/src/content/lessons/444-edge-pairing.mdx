Centers banked — skill two of two: **edge pairing**. A 4×4 edge slot holds
**two wing pieces**: 24 wings, each with exactly one partner. Join all twelve
pairs and the cube is, functionally, a 3×3.

## One move does all the work

Slice out with `Uw` — the top two layers turn together. Watch the middle band:

<Figure size="card" height={320} caption="Uw — the top two layers turn as one. Watch the middle band, and the top row of every side centre going with it.">

      watch the wings AND the top row of every side centre. Dimming the centres

  <TwistyPlayer alg="Uw" puzzle="4x4x4" anchor="start" />
</Figure>

Two things happen at once:

- The **upper wings** shift one slot around the cube — this is what carries a
  wing next to its partner.
- The **top row of every side center** shifts with them — your centers break.

That's a loan, not damage. `Uw'` puts every center piece straight back — as
long as every side face sits where it started when you close.

<Callout type="info">
  The rule while sliced out: `U` and `D` turns are always free — they never
  touch the borrowed center row. A side-face turn (`F`, `R`, `B`, `L`) spins
  that row off course, so return each side face to where it started before
  the closing `Uw'`. Lift–turn–lower patterns do this on their own: the lift
  and the lower cancel.
</Callout>

## The pairing loop

1. **Line up a match** — place a wing and its partner on neighboring slots of
   the middle band so one slice drives them together.
2. **Slice out** — `Uw`. The pair joins.
3. **Store it** — lift the finished pair into the top or bottom layer, turn
   that layer to carry the pair aside, then lower the face again. The lift
   cancels, and an unpaired edge drops into the slot the pair left.
4. **Repeat** — line up the next match the same way, store again.
5. **Slice back** — `Uw'`. Centers restore; stored pairs survive.

Storage is safe by construction: a pair lying in the top or bottom layer has
both wings in the same layer, so no turn — not even the closing `Uw'` — can
split it.

## The rhythm: 2, then 3–2–3

One slice per pair works but wastes moves. Chain instead — every stored pair
frees the slot for the next unpaired edge, so one slice-out feeds several
matches:

- **First pass: 2 pairs.** Learn the motion — join, store, join, store, close.
- **Then 3–2–3.** Three more passes: three pairs, then two, then three.

That's ten edges. The last two are special.

## The last two edges: the flip trick

With only two unpaired edges left there is no spare edge to feed the slot —
storing stops working. New exit:

1. Bring both unfinished edges to the front of the middle band, one left,
   one right.
2. **Slice out** — `Uw`. Nothing joins yet — the slice stages the wings for
   the return trip. One front slot now holds one wing of *each* edge,
   mismatched.
3. **Flip that edge in place** — lift it out of the band, roll it over the
   top, and reinsert it into the same slot, side faces back where they
   started. Flipping swaps its wings between the two inner layers — each now
   sits in its partner's path. There is an algorithm for exactly this, and it
   is worth learning as a string rather than improvising it every time:

   <AlgText moves="R U R' F R' F' R" />

   It turns the front-right edge over in place and moves the four top edges
   round by one, intact. Seven outer turns, nothing wide — so it cannot break
   a pair you have already made, and it means **literally the same thing on a
   5×5**, where you will meet it again.
4. **Slice back** — `Uw'` completes *both* edges at once and snaps the
   centers home.

<Callout type="tip">
  Pairing order does not matter — no edge is "first," and a pair stored on the
  bottom is worth exactly one stored on top. The only thing to protect is the
  centers: every `Uw` must meet its `Uw'`, and every side-face turn between
  them must be undone before you close.
</Callout>

Twelve edges closed: six one-color centers, twelve fat edges, eight corners.
Hold it like a 3×3 and solve. Along the way you should expect cases no 3×3
can produce — that's **parity**, it's normal, and it's the next lesson.
